**TL;DR:** Power Platform's advantage is gravity — it's already in your Microsoft tenant, on one identity and one invoice. Two structural facts decide the hard cases anyway. First, the cost is a **success tax**: every line item (per-user, per-GB of Dataverse, per-AI-message) scales with adoption, so the bill grows fastest exactly when the app is working. A realistic 500-user deployment with one grounded agent models to **~$216k/year at list**, and climbs with use. Second, there's an **exit asymmetry**: it's frictionless to enter because everything's already there, and expensive to leave because a solution export carries the app's *shape* but not your *data*, and the runtime can't leave Azure at all. For a sovereignty or cost-at-scale buyer, those two facts decide the platform before any feature comparison.

The honest starting point is that the gravity is real and rational. Power Platform reports ~56 million monthly active users; Microsoft says 80%+ of the Fortune 500 have built agents with Copilot Studio. If your company runs on Microsoft 365, the platform is *right there* — one Entra identity, native hooks into Teams, SharePoint, and Dynamics, one invoice, one support relationship. For a Microsoft-standardized shop, choosing it is often the correct call, and this article is not going to pretend otherwise.

It's going to do two things the demo never does: put a number on what "it's basically free, it's already in the tenant" becomes at scale, and show what happens the day you try to leave.

## The success tax: a worked model

Here is a concrete deployment, with every assumption stated so you can re-run it with your own. Five hundred users, a handful of apps, and one customer-facing agent. List prices, no enterprise discount:

| Line item | Assumption | Annual cost (list) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Power Apps Premium | 500 users × $20/user/mo | $120,000 |
| Dataverse storage | 50 GB beyond included × ~$40/GB/mo | $24,000 |
| Copilot Studio agent | 2,000 grounded msgs/day × ~10 credits × $0.01 | ~$72,000 |
| **Total** | | **~$216,000 / yr** |

Now read the *shape* of that table, which matters more than the total. Every line is **per-unit and scales with success**: more people using the apps (per-user), more records accumulating (per-GB), more questions asked of the agent (per-message, and RAG grounding is ~10 credits each, the expensive kind). Double the adoption and you roughly double the bill. The platform that was cheap as a pilot gets most expensive precisely when it's working — that's the **success tax**, and it's structural, not a pricing tier you can opt out of.

Two caveats in both directions. Your real number will be lower per-unit (volume and Enterprise Agreement discounts are real) — and also higher in ways the table omits (premium-connector scenarios, additional capacity, Power Automate flows, the $5 per-app plan that **retired for new customers on January 2, 2026**, pushing them to the $20 tier or pay-as-you-go). The point isn't the exact dollar figure; it's that a self-hosted runtime's marginal cost for one more user, one more GB, or one more agent call is *your own infrastructure* — roughly flat — while Power Platform's is a meter that ticks with every unit of success.

## The exit asymmetry

The second fact is the one that turns a cost problem into a trap. Power Platform "solutions" export, which sounds like portability. Read precisely what crosses the boundary:

> A solution export carries your **metadata** — tables, forms, flows, app definitions — but **not the data in your tables.**

So you can take the *shape* of your apps and must leave the *contents* behind. Your business data lives in **Dataverse**; you migrated it in to begin, and you pay ongoing storage to keep it there. And the runtime itself — the thing that executes the apps — is **Azure-only, with no self-hosting option, which Microsoft has stated it won't offer.** Leaving is therefore not a download; it's a re-platforming project: rebuild the app logic somewhere that can run it, export and reload every Dataverse table, and reconstruct the integrations.

![What you can and can't take out of Power Platform](./diagram-en.svg)

That's the asymmetry, stated plainly: **easy in, because everything is already there; hard out, because everything is now in there.** The same gravity that makes adoption frictionless is what makes exit a project — and you don't feel it until the day you have a reason to leave, by which point the app is the system 200 people use.

## The trigger that has no workaround: sovereignty

For one class of buyer the cost model and the exit asymmetry are beside the point, because a single fact ends the evaluation: **there is no self-hosted, on-prem, or air-gapped Power Platform.** If you're a bank under residency rules, a defense or government workload that must be air-gapped, or a healthcare system with sovereignty requirements, you cannot put the *runtime* where your regulators require it — not for a price, not on a roadmap. Everything else in this article is a trade-off; this one is a wall.

## The rebuttal, met honestly: "the gravity is worth it"

The strongest case for Power Platform is real and you should state it at full strength: *it's already in our tenant, it shares our identity and our invoice, it integrates natively with the Microsoft tools our people already live in, and consolidating on one vendor is worth a lot.* For a great many organizations that is simply true, and the lock-in is a trade they're rational to accept.

That case fails only on three triggers — and the discipline is to check whether any apply to *you*, not to argue the general case:

1. **Sovereignty** — you must self-host. No workaround exists; this alone decides it.
2. **Cost at scale** — your usage is large or growing fast enough that the success tax compounds past what owning a runtime would cost.
3. **Governing AI changes** — you need the changes Copilot makes to your apps to be reviewable diffs on a runtime you control, not opaque managed-solution config you can only inspect after the fact.

If none of the three apply, Microsoft's gravity is hard to beat and you should probably let it win. If even one applies, no amount of in-tenant convenience addresses it, because each is a property of *where the runtime lives and what it costs to grow*, not a feature Power Platform can ship.

## Where Power Platform is the right call — and the cost of the alternative

To keep this honest: if you're all-in on Microsoft, your data already lives in the Microsoft cloud, you have no sovereignty requirement, and you value one-throat-to-choke consolidation, Power Platform is an excellent, rational choice, and the lock-in may never cost you anything you care about. Plenty of internal apps never need to leave the tenant.

And the alternative isn't free. Self-hosting a runtime means *you* run it — patching, scaling, backups, the operational weight Microsoft otherwise carries for you. That's a real cost, and for a small team with no sovereignty need it can outweigh the success tax entirely. We won't pretend owning the runtime is strictly better; it's better *for the buyer who has one of the three triggers*, and overhead for the one who has none.
