A company was selecting an AI agent platform, and the shortlist came together naturally: Agentforce, Copilot Studio, ServiceNow AI Agents. All three are strong, all three came recommended.

They nearly signed with Agentforce — it demoed best for the CRM scenario. Right before the final step, an architect raised a question: "The customer issues we want the agent to handle — half need to look up opportunities in Salesforce, half need to look up delivery and tickets in our in-house systems. How does Agentforce reach the half outside the wall?"

Sales' answer was "we can do integrations." So they spent six weeks on an integration PoC. The conclusion was clear: it can connect, but every system outside the wall you add requires rewriting a sync, aligning definitions once more, and maintaining a pipeline that will break — and they had seven or eight systems outside the wall. In that moment the team realized: **the problem isn't that Agentforce isn't good enough — it's that, like the other two, it assumes a premise that doesn't hold for this company.**

The cost of six weeks and a PoC bought a lesson they could have reasoned through from the start.

## The Unspoken Premise: Your Whole World Lives Inside One Wall

The strength of these suites is essentially "**best within the ecosystem.**" Agentforce assumes your customers, processes, and objects are already in Salesforce; Copilot Studio assumes your collaboration, documents, and identity are in 365; ServiceNow assumes your processes are all on it. When the premise holds, they really are effortless — data ready, permissions ready, integrations ready, the agent connects almost on the first try.

But this premise doesn't hold for most enterprises. The real enterprise looks like this: CRM from one vendor, ERP from another, ticketing from a third, plus a pile of in-house systems and spreadsheets; they want to keep model choice open, because models change every year; they're under regulatory constraints, so data can't just leave the domain. For such an enterprise, "best within the ecosystem" becomes a constraint instead — because your world simply doesn't live inside any one vendor's wall. Having to rewrite a pipeline for every system outside the wall is exactly the wall that company hit in its PoC.

## Laid Out Side by Side: Not Which Is Better, But Which Suits Whom

| Dimension | Closed suite (Agentforce / Copilot / ServiceNow) | Open self-hosted runtime (ObjectStack / ObjectOS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | Business already heavily concentrated in that ecosystem | Data scattered across systems, cross-stack, must be self-held |
| Data location | Vendor cloud | Your own infrastructure |
| Model choice | Favors first-party or bound models | Switch freely — model outside, runtime inside |
| Ownership of business definition | Grows inside the platform | Open-protocol metadata in your repo (Apache 2.0) |
| Billing | Per-action / per-seat | Per runtime and infrastructure, decoupled from usage |
| Starting posture | Move the business into the ecosystem first | Extend existing systems, no migration required |

This table has no "all correct" column.

## First, Two Buckets of Cold Water: A Lot of the Time, You Should Choose the Suite

Let's say it up front, so this piece doesn't become a one-sided pitch. Open self-hosting isn't the more noble choice — it's the choice prepared for a specific situation. And many companies' situations are exactly suited to a suite.

**First, if your world really is basically inside one wall.** If 90% of your business is in Salesforce, you don't care about data leaving the domain, and you don't plan to switch models — then Agentforce is most likely the optimal answer. Taking on the real, present migration and operating burden to guard against the not-yet-happened risk of "lock-in" isn't worth it.

**Second, if you have no platform team.** This is the classic "build vs. buy." Open and self-hosted means you have to run it yourself — patching, guaranteeing availability, managing upgrades. The suite's "works out of the box, someone picks up the phone when something breaks, an SLA backstops it" managed experience is often, for a company without a dedicated platform team, more valuable and more relaxing than "complete control." Control has an operating cost, and control no one can carry is a burden.

## But "Buying a Suite" Saves Less Work Than You Think

Still, on the "buy" side, there's a bill often left out of the math: **the integration tax.**

The suite's quote lists the cost of seats or actions. What it doesn't list: each of your seven or eight systems outside the wall needs an integration pipeline — aligning definitions when you build it, then continuously maintaining it as both sides upgrade, and whichever end changes a field, the pipeline may silently break. That opening company's six-week PoC measured exactly the down payment on this tax. So "buy a suite + a pile of brittle integrations" is, on the surface, buy, but at its core you're still building — and building the least valuable, most brittle part.

Fold this integration tax into TCO, and the conclusion "buying is more relaxing" only holds when you have few systems outside the wall, or when those systems don't need to be touched by the agent in the first place. The more scattered your systems, the heavier this tax, and the more the suite's "relaxing" advantage gets nibbled away.

## Actually, You Can Use Both: Hybrid Isn't Fence-Sitting

This leads to an option long overlooked in selection debates: **this isn't necessarily a single-choice question.**

The most pragmatic architecture is often hybrid: in the ecosystem you genuinely use heavily, keep using its suite — let the sales agent inside Salesforce do what Agentforce does best; but on the layer that's **cross-system, needs unified definitions, and needs self-held data**, use an open, self-hosted runtime as the connecting tissue. The latter doesn't replace the suite — it does the thing the suite is structurally bad at: gathering business scattered across multiple walls into one unified definition that you hold yourself, with permissions and audit attached.

In other words, the open runtime can be the **connecting tissue** above your several systems (including those suites), rather than yet another wall asking you to move in. Acknowledge this, and selection shifts from "which vendor do I bet on" to "which part uses a suite, which part uses open" — which is what most real enterprises ultimately land on.

## Open Self-Hosting — What Exactly Do You Get

If you belong in the right-hand column, or if what you need to build is precisely that cross-system connecting layer, then "open + self-hosted" buys you not sentiment, but concrete capability. What's most telling is its posture toward existing systems — which is also where that opening company got stuck.

The suite wants your world to move into its wall; the open runtime assumes your world is scattered to begin with, and its job is to make existing systems natively support AI rather than migrate first. That company's customer data was half in Salesforce, half in in-house systems. On ObjectOS, it can use one definition to gather both sides into the same "customer":

```ts
export const Customer = ObjectSchema.create({
  name: 'crm_customer',
  label: 'Customer',
  sources: [
    Source.salesforce('Account'),        // The opportunity half lives here
    Source.database('legacy.customers'),  // The delivery and ticket half lives here
  ],
  identity: ['tax_id'],                  // Use one rule to decide "the same customer"
});
```

The difference can be made concrete down to a single question. "Is this customer worth committing resources to retain?" —

- **Before**: the agent connected to Salesforce, saw active opportunities, answered "worth it"; it had no idea that in the in-house system this customer's deliveries kept slipping and tickets were piling up.
- **After**: the agent faces **one** unified customer, seeing opportunities, delivery, and tickets together, and answers "the opportunities look good, but delivery risk is high — fix fulfillment first before talking expansion."

Same model, same question; only because the "customer" beneath it is no longer sliced by walls, the conclusion goes from partial to whole. And none of this requires rewriting a sync pipeline for each system outside the wall — the business definition is diffable, migratable metadata in your repository; the runtime runs on your own infrastructure, enforcing permissions and recording audit; the model can come from any external vendor. You've handed "the definition of your own business" to no one for safekeeping.

## An Honest Multiple-Choice Question

So this isn't "who should be eliminated," it's "which part goes to whom." Ask yourself three questions:

1. Is my business basically all inside one ecosystem, or scattered across multiple systems?
2. Can I accept data flowing continuously to the vendor cloud? Can I sign off on that for compliance?
3. Do I **have a team** to operate a self-hosted runtime?

If the first two answers lean "scattered, no," and the third leans "yes" — then what you're looking for was never another stronger closed suite, at least not on that cross-system layer. If all three answers lean the other way, then go ahead and use the suite generously; they really are easy to use. Honestly slotting yourself into the right category — even generously mixing the two — matters far more than chasing whose features are newer. And it's far cheaper than spending six weeks on a PoC only to figure it out.

## Closing

What closed suites win on is "best within the ecosystem," an advantage that's very real when its premise holds and shouldn't be denied. What open self-hosting wins on is "still holding when the premise doesn't" — when your world is scattered, your data must be self-held, your model must be free, and your definition must be held in your own hands, and you can bear the responsibility of running it yourself.

Knowing which part should use a suite, which part should use open, and what each costs you — matters more than any vendor's feature list. Had that opening company asked those three questions sooner, it could have saved six weeks and a PoC.
