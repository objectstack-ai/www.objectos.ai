At quarter's end, the CFO opens the agent platform's bill and pauses: it's four times the budget.

No one overspent — quite the opposite. The customer-service agent was simply too successful. Lay the numbers out: before launch, the team handled about 5,000 tickets a day; after the agent went live, per-person efficiency rose and throughput doubled to about 10,000. And the platform bills per action — for every ticket handled, there's looking up the customer, looking up the order, looking up history, querying the knowledge base, issuing a refund, sending a notification: seven or eight actions to start. Rough math: 10,000 tickets × 7 actions × $0.10 ≈ $7,000 a day, about $2.5 million a year. The budget, meanwhile, had been guessed at "5,000 tickets, fewer per ticket."

Here's the most ironic part: **the more successful this project is, the more frightening this bill gets.** So the board's first reaction isn't to celebrate doubled efficiency — it's to ask "can we throttle the usage?" A success that should be scaled gets the brakes slammed on it because of the billing model. Before the ROI has truly materialized, the scale is already choked by cost anxiety.

## A Dime, Multiplied by a Number That Keeps Inflating

Salesforce's Agentforce rolled out Flex billing: $0.10 per action. Look at the number alone and it's so cheap it lulls you into letting your guard down.

But this is exactly the thing to watch most in agent billing — it ties cost to **usage**, and the entire point of an agent is to act autonomously, frequently, and tirelessly. And the more autonomous the agent, the more intermediate actions it fires to do one thing well: a request to "tidy up this customer's situation for me" might be a dozen-plus tool calls behind the scenes — which you can neither count nor cap easily in advance. Some platforms bill per token, even harder to predict than per action — for the same task, a few extra reasoning steps and the bill grows another notch. The comparison in public reporting isn't exaggerated: at equal consumption, the SaaS model's annual cost can reach **over $2 million**, while a functionally equivalent self-hosted deployment — license plus infrastructure — is on the order of **$250,000**. That's an order of magnitude apart.

## Isn't Pay-As-You-Go Cheaper and More Flexible?

Here we have to seriously take on a rebuttal, because in many scenarios it's correct: pay-as-you-go has no upfront investment, you spend less when you use less, the vendor runs ops for you, and you only pay for the value produced — this logic was validated over and over in the cloud era, so why wouldn't it hold for agents?

It holds, but with one precondition: **the "quantity" that cost grows with must be decoupled from, or weakly correlated with, your value.** Cloud storage billed per GB is fine, because storing more usually means a bigger business that can afford it; the two track roughly in sync.

Agents break this precondition. They bill per "action count," and action count surges precisely with **degree of autonomy** and **degree of success** — the more you want it to do for you and the better you want it done, the more actions it fires and the higher the bill. This becomes a kind of **tax on success**: the harder you optimize (having the agent check one more step, verify once more to raise quality), the higher the tax rate. Traditional software bills per seat, and however hard you use it, cost is capped; bill per action, and the better you do, the more you pay. That cloud-era intuition of "pay-as-you-go is fair" fails on an execution entity that fires countless intermediate actions on its own — and that you encourage to do more.

## So How Much Usage Justifies Running It Yourself? Compute the Crossover

Don't stop at "it depends." Using the CFO's two numbers, you can roughly compute the crossover point.

On the self-hosting side, assume annual cost is fixed at about $250,000 (license + infrastructure + a share of ops), and it **barely varies with call count.** On the per-action side it's $0.10 × number of actions. Set the two equal:

```text
$250,000 = $0.10 × annual actions
annual actions ≈ 2.5 million  →  about 6,800 actions per day
```

In other words, once your daily agent actions exceed the ~**7,000** range, per-action billing starts costing more than self-hosting, and the gap widens from there. Back to that CFO: at 70,000 actions a day (10,000 tickets × 7), he's long been **ten times** past the crossover — which is exactly why the bill flew to $2.5 million. His pain wasn't an accident; he **should have been running it himself long ago**, except the billing model locked him on the wrong side.

You can apply this formula directly to your own numbers: multiply your team's daily interaction volume by "roughly how many actions per interaction," compare against the ~7,000 mark, and you'll know which side of the crossover you're on.

## Three Cost Models, Laid Out Side by Side

| Model | Representative | What cost grows with | Predictability | Where the data is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Per-action / per-token | Agentforce Flex ($0.10/action) | Inflates linearly or even super-linearly with action count | Poor — the better you use it, the more expensive | Vendor cloud |
| Per-seat | Most Copilot-class | Grows with headcount, capped but not low per person | Medium, but decoupled from agent autonomy | Vendor cloud |
| Self-hosted runtime | ObjectStack / ObjectOS | Grows with infrastructure, basically decoupled from usage | Good — double the usage and the curve barely moves | Your own infrastructure |

Per-seat looks stable, but it has an inherent contradiction: an agent's value lies in replacing a large volume of repetitive labor without taking a seat, yet you're still paying per "head" — the billing dimension and the value dimension don't line up. Self-hosting swaps out the entire cost structure: you pay for the execution engine and your own compute, and when usage goes from ten thousand to a hundred thousand, the bill doesn't ten-x along with it.

## First, Some Cold Water: Self-Hosting Isn't Cheaper Everywhere

Don't take "save 40–60%" as universally true. It has a definite range where it holds, and crossing the boundary breaks it.

That $250,000 headline number **doesn't count people.** Self-hosting means you need someone to run ops, patch, and guarantee availability — if you have no platform team, this hidden labor cost can eat a large chunk of the paper advantage, and also push that crossover point to the right. And it has fixed costs: infrastructure and ops are spent up front, with little relation to usage.

So in the very-small, very-low-frequency, purely experimental stage, pay-as-you-go is actually more flexible and cheaper — you don't have to keep a team to run a nearly idle runtime. Self-hosting's advantage only materializes once you're **certain you'll reach scale.**

## The Truly Expensive Line Item Isn't on the Price List

Even once you're on the side where self-hosting pays off, comparing only the monthly bill still underestimates the problem. Before picking a billing model, what you should truly compute is **three bills**, two of which aren't on the price list:

1. **The usage bill: will cost surge with success?** This is the CFO's four-times bill — whether your usage is positively correlated with the agent's success. If yes, per-action is a trap.
2. **The compliance bill: the cost of data leaving your domain.** Per-action and per-seat SaaS means business data flows continuously to the vendor cloud. With the EU AI Act enforcing in August and CADA proposing a sovereignty framework, the penalty for a single data-residency violation could pay for many years of license.
3. **The lock-in bill: the cost of not being able to leave later.** When business definitions, processes, and permissions all grow inside one platform, migration cost compounds with time in use. You think you're paying for software; you're actually putting down a deposit on "not being able to leave later."

Add these three together and the surface cheapness of "a dime an action" gets eaten up fast.

## Why Self-Hosting Can Pin All These Down at Once

The core is just one line: **you pay for the engine that runs this business definition, not for how many times it gets called.** From this:

- **Cost decoupled from usage.** ObjectOS executes the business definition on your own servers, and however many times the agent calls a governed tool, it's not billed per call — cost is determined by infrastructure: predictable and plannable. That CFO's tickets double and the bill barely moves.
- **Data stays in domain.** Objects, permissions, and audit evidence all stay on your own infrastructure, so compliance risk and the cost of data leaving your domain drop together.
- **No lock-in.** The business definition is metadata in your repository under an open protocol (Apache 2.0): diffable, migratable. What you buy is the runtime's service, not a deposit on "not being able to leave later."

## Closing

"$0.10 per action" is a carefully designed price tag: it looks harmless when you're just starting out with low usage, and bares its fangs when you're truly successful and usage takes off. Back to that CFO at quarter's end — his bill wasn't out of control, it was the inevitable result this billing model was designed to produce: it handed him the biggest bill at the very moment he should have been celebrating.

When you do the math on agent cost, don't just look at unit price. Compute those three bills — usage, compliance, lock-in — then use that crossover formula to compare against your own usage. Work all this out, and "self-hosting saves 40–60%" stops being a slogan and becomes a conclusion you can write into the budget — one you also know when *not* to count on.
