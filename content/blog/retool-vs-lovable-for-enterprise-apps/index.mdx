Choose by who will change the app. For engineers building on existing databases, pick Retool: its AI stays within access you have granted and its audit log starts on its Business plan, but on Free and Team its All Users group can edit every app, and pull-request review takes its top plan. For business teams generating whole apps, pick Lovable: it builds and hosts the app, its Git sync allows pull-request review on every plan, and its top plan can hold public apps for an admin's or owner's approval. But its AI writes the data-access rules, which do not bind the app's privileged server account. Either way, someone answers for what the AI built.

*Every Retool and Lovable claim below is quoted from that vendor's public docs or pricing page, read on 17 November 2026. The ObjectStack results were measured on ObjectStack 18.5.0, the version a new install gets today. Sources and transcripts are in [The proof](#the-proof).*

## The question behind the comparison

If you searched for this comparison, you have probably already accepted that an AI will build the app. The open question is what happens after it does. Three requests will reach the IT lead, usually in this order: a business owner wants a change, an auditor asks who approved the last one, and someone asks what the app did to a particular record on a particular day. So the useful comparison is not how fast each tool builds. It is where in each tool those three requests get answered, and by whom.

Retool's docs describe an app builder where "the primary development experience of apps is by prompting an AI agent with natural language", working with the databases and services your company already connects to Retool. This post describes that AI app builder, which Retool calls Apps, not its classic drag-and-drop apps. Lovable's docs describe a tool that generates the whole app (screens, server and database) and by default hosts it. We covered each one in [the Retool post](/en/blog/retool-vs-ai-native-app-platform/) and [the Lovable post](/en/blog/is-lovable-safe-for-production/), and more platforms on our [compare page](/en/compare/).

## Seven questions, two vendors

Each cell says what the vendor's own docs or pricing page say. A bold tag names the plan a feature needs where the vendor limits it. Three terms come up: a **pull request** is a proposed change, in your code repository (for example GitHub), that someone approves before it takes effect; **Git sync** is Lovable's two-way copy of an app's code into your code repository; **MCP** is the protocol AI assistants use to connect to other software.

| The question you will be asked | Retool | Lovable |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Who can change the app?** | Builders, who can also publish; and "internal users" (employees who use apps), who can create apps but not publish them. **Free, Team:** fixed groups; All Users can edit every app. | Editors and above. Workspace members "can see, edit, and publish the workspace's projects". **Business, Enterprise:** a project can be restricted to invited people. |
| **How is a change reviewed before users see it?** | Changes stay in a separate working copy with its own preview. The AI asks before running anything that writes data. | Drafts show "a one-line summary of what the draft changes" and the edits before acceptance. **All plans:** Git sync can add pull-request review. |
| **Who approves?** | A builder publishes; an internal user's app needs a builder. **Enterprise:** for an app under source control, merging a pull request publishes, with an app preview for reviewers. | By default, "editors and above can publish". **Enterprise:** policies can require an admin or owner to approve public apps, apps using connectors, or outside collaborators' publishing. |
| **What permissions does the running app enforce?** | Users need access to a data source to run its queries. **Business, Enterprise:** per-group control. On PostgreSQL resources with enforcement turned on, policies apply to every query, "including … agents". | Database rules its AI writes; you read them and change them through chat. They do not apply to the app's privileged server account (`service_role`). A scan on every publish "checks database access rules". |
| **What does the audit log record?** | **Business, Enterprise:** every query run and user actions in Retool, with MCP clients tagged. Kept one year on Retool's cloud. **Enterprise:** streaming to Datadog or Splunk. | **Enterprise:** workspace activity, including members, projects, prompts and publishes. Kept 13 weeks; scheduled export to your security-monitoring system. |
| **Where do the data and the definition live?** | Apps use your connected databases and services; the app lives in Retool. Enterprise card: "Retool Cloud, self-hosted, or air-gapped" (air-gapped: no internet connection). | Code in "Lovable's managed infrastructure" by default; Git sync (all plans) can keep a copy in your repository. Database: Lovable's, or one you connect, such as Supabase. |
| **Where does it stop, and how do you leave?** | Running an exported AI-builder app outside Retool is listed as not yet supported (classic apps may differ). **Enterprise:** an app placed under source control keeps its source in your repository. | Docs: "you are never locked in"; code and data can be exported. Accepting a draft "can change your live app's backend immediately". Backend logs (separate from the audit log) "do not prove who made a particular database change". |

What the table shows:

- **Approval happens at a different step.** In Retool it is publishing, or on Enterprise the merge of a pull request. In Lovable it is accepting a draft and then publishing, with an admin's or owner's approval on Enterprise for the kinds of publishing a policy names. The two differ in what the approver is shown.
- **The two audit logs answer different questions.** Retool's log records user actions in Retool, "such as query runs and password resets". Lovable's log records workspace activity; its listed events include "Prompt sent", "Project published externally" and "Database query executed". Write down your auditor's question first, then check which log answers it.
- **The controls cost a higher plan.** Retool's pricing page lists rich permission controls and audit logs from Business, and Git source control on Enterprise only; Lovable's docs mark audit logs and publishing approval as Enterprise only.

## When Retool is the better choice

Pick Retool when the apps are internal tools on systems you already run, and the people building them are engineers or technical staff. Its strongest point for an IT lead is that the AI never gets more reach than the builder has: its security page says "the LLM cannot read or write any resource data the user is not already authorized to access", and the AI asks the builder before it runs anything that writes, inserts, updates or deletes data. Its audit log records each query an app's users run, with the exact query and its parameters, and tags actions from AI assistants connected through Retool's MCP server. If your auditors ask what a user did in an internal tool, that log answers directly. The limit to know: running an app made in its AI app builder outside Retool is listed as not yet supported. Budget for Business if you need per-group permissions and the audit log, and for Enterprise if every change must go through a pull request in your own repository.

## When Lovable is the better choice

Pick Lovable when business teams need to go from an idea to a working app on their own, and the app can live on a server and database that Lovable runs for you. It builds the screens, server and database together, hosts them, and lets people try changes in a draft before accepting them. Its Git sync works on every plan, so your developers can send its changes through the same pull-request review they already use; publishing the app stays a separate step. Lovable offers single sign-on from its Business plan, where Retool lists it on Enterprise only. Budget for Enterprise if you want an admin or owner to approve before an app is made public, uses a connector, or is published by an outside collaborator; the audit log and scheduled security scans are Enterprise too. Its docs make a plain promise about leaving: "you are never locked in. You can export your code, migrate your data, self-host parts or all of your stack, or move to another provider at any time." The same page lists what you take on if you do leave, including "Implementing authentication and enforcing data isolation policies".

## What we measured on ObjectStack

ObjectStack, which we build, takes a third approach: the app's definition is a set of plain files, and the running app enforces what they say. We ran these checks on the open-source CRM example that ships with ObjectStack 18.5.0, a demonstration app rather than a customer system. The sales-rep role may read, create and edit deals, but not delete them.

| What we did | What the system answered | What it means for you |
|:--|:--|:--|
| An AI agent, using an access key created for a sales-rep account, tried to delete a deal through MCP. | Refused twice: first because no person had confirmed a permanent delete, then, with the confirmation, because sales reps may not delete deals. | A permanent delete needs a person's confirmation, and even then the agent gets only the rights of the account it uses. |
| A sales rep tried the same delete through the app's API. | Refused. | The rule holds for people and agents alike. |
| The agent moved the deal from "prospecting" to "qualification", which sales reps may do. | One audit row: the agent's account, the deal, and the stage before and after. The refused deletes left no row. | You can show what an agent changed and when. Refusals are only in the server log. |
| The sales rep asked to read the audit log. | Refused. | A sales rep cannot read who did what; reading the log takes a role that grants it. |
| An AI agent changed the app's files so the sales role could delete deals. | The platform's first check of the files passed. The build stopped with one line naming the change, and stayed stopped until the accept command was run (in our run, by the same agent; see the limits below). | The change cannot take effect unseen, but a person still has to review the one-line change that accepting it produces. |

The last check is the mechanism behind [Should your business app live in a database or a repository?](/en/blog/where-should-a-business-app-definition-live/) and [How do you catch an AI's silent permission change before it ships?](/en/blog/catch-ai-silent-permission-change-access-matrix-gate/), which explain it in full. How opening a record is audited, and the limits of that record, are in [Can you prove who opened a customer record, including an AI agent?](/en/blog/prove-who-opened-a-customer-record-view-auditing/)

## What ObjectStack does not do

- **It does not judge whether a change is right.** The platform's first check passed the delete permission without comment. The build stopped only because this example app keeps an access table. A new app has no such table until you switch it on, and the automatic checks a new project ships with run the first check, not the build.
- **It does not stop the agent from accepting its own change.** Accepting is one command that anyone, or anything, with access to the files can run. It becomes a review only when that one-line change goes through your pull-request process with a person's approval.
- **It does not record refused attempts in the audit log.** Both refused deletes appear only in the server's log, a separate place from the audit log.
- **It does not know an agent from a person.** The audit row names the account the agent's access key belongs to, and nothing on the row says "AI". Give each agent its own account, or the log will name a person for what the agent did.
- **It does not keep every change in files.** A running environment can also hold edits made in its admin screens or through its API, and those edits take precedence over the files. Which changes must go through the files is a policy you set.
- **It is younger than both vendors.** ObjectStack's own comparison page says: "ObjectStack is younger; expect to build more of it yourself."

## How to choose

Retool and Lovable each fit the cases described above. Look at a third option if the person who answers for the app must read who-can-do-what as a short table rather than as code, and must see every change to it before it takes effect. That is the case ObjectStack is built for, with the limits listed above; to try it on your own process, [point your agent at ObjectStack](/en/blog/how-to-point-your-agent-at-objectstack/).

Whichever you choose, ask the vendor three questions and keep the answers:

1. When the AI changes who can see or do what, what exactly does the approver see before users get it?
2. Can the person who approves be someone other than the person who made the change?
3. Which log answers "what did this user, or this agent, do to that record", and for how long is it kept?

### 4. A new project has no access table

The `create-objectstack@18.5.0` package, which `npm create objectstack@latest` runs today, contains no `access-matrix.json` in its template, and the template's CI workflow runs `pnpm validate`, not the build. The first `os compile --update-access-matrix` in a new project is the opt-in.
