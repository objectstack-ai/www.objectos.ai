Let's start with how one company lost a customer over exactly this. The details are anonymized, but you've probably seen every step before.

Yuanfeng — the name we'll give this industrial-equipment manufacturer — does a few billion in annual revenue and serves a few thousand customers. In 2024, it bet its data foundation on Microsoft, building a clean ontology in Fabric: what a "customer" is, which "devices" connect to a customer, which "work orders" hang off each device — all defined crisply. That year it was the model case named over and over at vendor conferences.

In 2026, three things hit Yuanfeng almost at once:

- The data-science team wanted to run a batch of renewal predictions on Gemini, because in that specific scenario it really was more accurate;
- Compliance got notice that EU customer data had to stay in the EU and could no longer touch the US cloud;
- An acquisition closed, bringing in an entire sales organization — several thousand customers — running on Salesforce.

So now Yuanfeng had **three "customers"**: one in Fabric, one in Salesforce, and one more in the compliance-isolated EU environment.

The turning point came over a key account we'll call H Group. One day the sales director asked an agent, "How high is H Group's renewal risk next year?" The agent answered, "Low." It was reading the Fabric ontology, where H Group's recent orders looked healthy and the numbers were pretty.

But what the agent didn't see: in the Salesforce records (brought in by the acquisition), H Group had escalated complaints to the executive level twice in six months; in the EU-isolated environment, there was a 90-day-overdue payment dispute hanging open. Three sets of data belonged to three mutually unaware "customers," and **no layer anywhere knew they were all the same H Group**.

A quarter later, H Group churned — millions in annual loss. The post-mortem was stark enough to leave the room silent: the agent had not technically erred. The slice of data it saw genuinely showed low risk. What was wrong wasn't the model. It was the definition of "customer" beneath its feet, sliced into three.

This wasn't a Yuanfeng mistake. It's the inevitable result of handing "the definition of your business" to a platform for safekeeping — and each platform only safekeeps its own slice.

## Not an Isolated Case: Everyone Is Racing for This Layer

Yuanfeng's predicament is common precisely because the whole industry is rushing toward the same layer at once.

A year ago, "business semantic layer / Ontology" was basically Palantir's proprietary vocabulary. This year, Gartner positioned the semantic layer and knowledge graphs directly as the infrastructure of agentic AI at its Data & Analytics Summit; forecasts for the enterprise knowledge graph market run from roughly $1.9 billion today to nearly $10 billion by 2032. The players are all in too: Microsoft pushing Fabric IQ, Google putting enterprise knowledge graphs front and center, Palantir selling Foundry while binding NVIDIA's compute.

Why now, of all moments? Because agents punched a hole in it. The data pipelines of the last decade only cared about *where the data flowed*, not so much *what the data meant* — there was always a human as the backstop. Agents have no such backstop: confronted with dozens of tables and "customer" fields defined a dozen different ways, they either answer wrong (as with H Group) or don't dare answer at all. Those glaring industry numbers — 88% of agent pilots never reach production — die in large part on exactly this missing layer.

So the three giants' judgment is right, and remarkably aligned: **for AI to reliably enter the enterprise, there must first be a layer of machine-readable, governed business definition.** That point no longer needs arguing. What does need arguing is the next question — who this layer should belong to.

## Three Right Directions, Three Different Locks

Lay the three approaches side by side and you run into an awkward common thread.

| | Where the ontology lives | Who can read it | Can you take it with you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Palantir Foundry | Inside the Palantir platform | On-site engineers + platform tooling | Bound to the platform; migration = rebuild |
| Microsoft Fabric IQ | Inside OneLake / Fabric | The 365 and Fabric ecosystems | Bound to the Microsoft stack |
| Google enterprise knowledge graph | Inside Google Cloud | The Vertex and GCP ecosystems | Bound to the Google stack |

To be fair, each one does well on its own turf. Palantir's ontology modeling quality is genuinely high; Microsoft welds the semantic layer into OneLake and 365, which for an all-Microsoft company is so smooth there's almost no friction; Google has real chops in data scale and graph reasoning. They're all helping you get your business clearly organized — and then locking that clarity into their own platform.

An old-school IT person reading this would reflexively say: vendor lock-in again, same old problem.

But this time the thing to worry about most isn't lock-in. This time there's a worse new problem. Before we take it apart, we have to lay out — honestly — the playbook by which closed platforms **win**. Because it isn't weak.

## First, the Playbook by Which Closed Platforms Win

If all you can do is chant "open is good," that's preaching, not analysis. Closed platforms hold four real cards.

**First, modeling quality.** Chewing twenty years of a large enterprise's accumulated mess into a clean, self-consistent ontology is extremely heavy engineering. Palantir aligns it one concept at a time with on-site engineers, at a quality open-source communities can't match in the short term. With an ontology, building it badly is worse than not building it at all.

**Second, a single accountable party.** When something breaks, someone picks up the phone, there's an SLA, there's a contract backstopping it. For a CIO, "one vendor carries the responsibility for the whole layer" is worth money in itself — this is what they call one throat to choke.

**Third, a lot of companies really are "basically on one stack."** If 80% of your business already lives in one ecosystem, then "best within the ecosystem" is, literally, the best option for you — the benefits of openness are ones you simply can't use.

**Fourth, integration depth.** Within one ecosystem, data, identity, and permissions are all already there; the dirty work it spares you is measured in person-months.

All four cards are real. So the conclusion is not "closed platforms are all traps" — for companies that sit inside their premises, they're often the right answer. The problem shows up in companies like Yuanfeng: their premises are **expiring.**

## This Time, the Playbook Has a New Hole: Fragmentation

With single-vendor lock-in, at least your business definition is still **one complete copy** — you just can't move it. Painful, but whole.

Three giants each building closed ontologies produces something else: **fragmentation.** Yuanfeng's "customer" wasn't locked up — it was sliced into three, lying in three mutually unaware platforms. This is far more painful than lock-in, and it's painful for good reason — two mechanisms guarantee it won't heal on its own.

**The first layer is incentives.** You might think: just let Microsoft's ontology understand Salesforce's "customer," problem solved. It won't, because no vendor has any incentive to do it. Each one's ontology is its moat. If Microsoft unilaterally unified its "customer" semantics with Salesforce's, that would be helping a rival move data more smoothly and commoditizing itself. **Unification is self-harm for every contestant in the race.** This isn't technical oversight; it's rational choice — so counting on any one of them to clean up the fragmentation is counting on something that won't happen.

**The second layer is technical.** Even setting incentives aside, cross-ontology semantic alignment is itself an unsolved problem: does System A's "customer" equal System B's "Account"? The field definitions, lifecycles, dedup rules, and what counts as "the same entity" all differ across the two sides. AI can't reliably do this automatically either — quite the opposite, agents err precisely because this layer of definite definition is missing. Back to H Group: let the agent guess on its own whether "these three records are the same company," and the cost of guessing wrong is exactly those millions.

Put together in one line: **you thought you bought three tools; you actually bought three mutually unaware sources of truth.** The more systems, the more acquisitions, the more compliance isolation, the more severe this fragmentation — and the agent era amplifies its cost precisely because the human brain can still, just barely, manually reconcile across three systems while an agent cannot. An agent needs one definite, cross-system-consistent layer of definition before it can reason.

For fragmentation not to happen, logically there's only one way out: **the definition of your business cannot belong to any platform competing in the race; it must be a neutral layer** — a definition you hold yourself, that all three vendors' tools can read. Closed platforms structurally can't provide this: they are contestants in the race, and they can't be referees at the same time.

## How to Tell You're Being Fragmented

This isn't abstract; it has very concrete early symptoms. Check yourself against the list below — three or more, and fragmentation is already happening inside your company.

1. The same "customer / order / device" is defined differently across systems and doesn't reconcile; every report requires manual stitching.
2. Ask an agent a cross-system question and it either equivocates or gets it half-right (saw one system, missed the other).
3. Every time you connect a new system, you have to re-teach the AI "what this is, what the fields mean."
4. An acquisition closed over a year ago and the two sides' master data still hasn't truly merged; each reports its own.
5. Compliance requires isolating a class of data, so the same entity is forced into several copies, none recognizing the other.

Before Yuanfeng lost H Group, four of these five were true. At the time they were all filed as "data governance to-dos," and no one realized they were actually a landmine an agent would eventually set off.

## First, Some Cold Water: Open Is Not a Silver Bullet

Here we have to honestly stop, or this turns into a sales pitch.

First, **swapping the definition for an open protocol does not automatically merge Yuanfeng's three "customers" into one.** The semantic modeling, dedup, and definition alignment that need doing still need doing — there's no silver bullet here, and don't believe anyone who claims one. What open really changes is the **ownership** of this hard work: the definition you sweat to align today is written in your own repository, not sedimented in some platform's backend. Next year, when you switch models, switch clouds, or get acquired, what you rebuild is the connections, not the definition itself.

Second, **"open" itself doesn't guarantee winning.** Historically, for an open standard to truly prevail it usually also needs a good-enough reference implementation plus an active ecosystem — publishing the protocol while no one makes it pleasant to use will fizzle just the same. So choosing open is a bet that "someone will build it solidly" — a judgment with execution risk, not a sure win.

But note the weight of these two buckets of cold water: they say "open also takes effort and also carries risk," not "closed is better." By comparison, the downside risk of locking your business definition into one platform — the fragmentation Yuanfeng suffered — is far larger and far harder to reverse. Neither side is free; it's just that one of them keeps the most core asset in your own hands.

## Layers Everyone Depends On End Up Neutral

The pattern itself isn't new, but it's worth telling with fresh examples, because it keeps happening.

The "definition layer" that an entire ecosystem collectively depends on always ends up neutral. The oldest example is SQL: database vendors fought to a bloody draw, yet the query language itself is public. More recent — and more telling — are these two: **OpenTelemetry**, the data definition for observability, which every monitoring vendor connects to, hosted by the neutral CNCF so no one can monopolize it; and **LSP (the Language Server Protocol)**, opened by Microsoft itself, yet precisely because it's open, adopted by every editor and turned into the de facto standard.

The LSP example is especially worth chewing on, because it's a thing Microsoft proved **with its own hands**: opening the definition layer and making money on the best implementation wins more than locking it down. The business semantic layer is the same kind of thing — in the future it will be depended on **simultaneously** by your applications, your agents, your audit systems, plus the tools of all three vendors. A layer depended on by so many parties cannot logically stay private to any one of them for long. Either it becomes neutral, or it keeps producing the fragmentation Yuanfeng suffered.

## What the Neutral Layer Looks Like

After all this, take a look at the real thing. The point isn't the syntax — it's **where it lives, whether you can take it with you, and whether it can pull the fragmentation back in.**

Suppose Yuanfeng had originally built "customer" as one neutral definition. With a single declaration, it could gather data scattered across three systems into the same object:

```ts
export const Customer = ObjectSchema.create({
  name: 'crm_customer',
  label: 'Customer',
  sources: [
    Source.fabric('Account'),              // Orders and devices live here
    Source.salesforce('Account'),          // Sales records from the acquisition live here
    Source.database('eu.customers'),       // Compliance-isolated EU data lives here
  ],
  identity: ['tax_id'],                    // Use one rule to decide "the same customer"
});
```

This definition lives in your Git repository: diffable, reviewable, migratable. What it can do next is the crux — turning "portable" into a demonstrable action, not a promise:

```bash

# Then point any model at it — Claude, GPT, Gemini — the runtime doesn't change
```

Now ask that fatal question again: "How high is H Group's renewal risk next year?" The agent now faces **one** unified "customer," with permissions and audit attached: orders healthy, but overlaid with two executive-level complaints and a 90-day payment dispute — and it answers, "High risk; recommend early intervention." Same model, same question; only because the definition beneath it is no longer fragmented, the conclusion shifts from "lost millions" to "warned a full quarter early."
