Choose by who will change the app. If engineers build internal tools on your existing databases, pick Retool: its AI works inside the access you have already granted, and on its top plan every change can go through a pull request in your own repository. If business teams will generate whole apps, pick Lovable: its top plan can hold publishing until an admin approves, but the data rules are code its AI wrote, and its docs say its app logs alone do not prove who changed your data. Either way, someone on your team answers for what the AI built.

*Every Retool and Lovable claim below is quoted from that vendor's public docs or pricing page, read on 17 November 2026. The ObjectStack column was measured on ObjectStack 18.5.0, the version a new install gets today. Sources and transcripts are in [The proof](#the-proof).*

## The question behind the comparison

If you searched for this comparison, you have probably already accepted that an AI will build the app. The open question is what happens after it does. Three requests will reach the IT lead, usually in this order: a business owner wants a change, an auditor asks who approved the last one, and someone asks what the app did to a record last Tuesday. So the useful comparison is not about how fast each tool builds. It is about where in each tool those three requests get answered, and by whom.

Retool and Lovable answer them in different places, because they are different products. Retool's own docs describe an app builder where "the primary development experience of apps is by prompting an AI agent with natural language", on top of the databases and APIs your company already connects to Retool. Lovable's docs describe a tool that generates the whole app, front end and back end, and by default hosts both. We covered each one separately in [the Retool post](/en/blog/retool-vs-ai-native-app-platform/) and [the Lovable post](/en/blog/is-lovable-safe-for-production/). This post puts them side by side on one checklist, with ObjectStack as a third column because we build it and measured it. A wider comparison is on [our compare page](/en/compare/).

Three things stand out once the answers sit next to each other.

**Approval sits in a different place in each tool.** In Retool it is the moment a builder publishes, or, on Enterprise with Source Control, the merge of a pull request. In Lovable it is accepting a draft, then publishing, and on Enterprise an admin's sign-off for the kinds of app a policy names. In ObjectStack it is accepting a change to a saved table of who may do what. None of the three decides for you whether the change is right. They differ in what the approver is shown.

**The audit log answers a different question in each tool.** Retool's log follows the people using the app, down to the query they ran. Lovable's log follows activity in the workspace where apps are built. ObjectStack's log follows changes to records, and an agent appears in it under the identity its key was issued to. Before you pick, write down the question your auditor will ask, and check which log answers it.

**Governance features sit on top plans.** Retool's pricing page lists audit logs from Business and Git source control on Enterprise only. Lovable's docs mark audit logs and publishing approval as Enterprise only. Price the plan that has the controls you need, not the entry plan.

## When Retool is the better choice

Pick Retool when the apps are internal tools on systems you already run, and when the people building them are engineers or technical staff. Its strongest point for an IT lead is that the AI never gets more reach than the builder has: Retool's security page says "the LLM cannot read or write any resource data the user is not already authorized to access", and the AI asks the builder before it runs anything that writes, inserts, updates or deletes data. On Enterprise, protected apps go through a pull request in your own repository, so the review step is the one your engineering team already uses. Its audit log follows end users down to the query they ran, and tags actions from AI clients. If your auditors ask what a user did in an internal tool, Retool's log has a direct answer.

## When Lovable is the better choice

Pick Lovable when business teams need to go from an idea to a working app on their own, and speed matters more than fitting an existing data estate. It builds the front end and the back end together, hosts both, and lets people try changes in a draft before accepting them. Its Business plan adds single sign-on, and its Enterprise plan adds the other controls an IT lead asks for first: scheduled security scans across every project, audit logs, and policies that hold publishing for an admin's approval. Its docs also make an unusually plain promise about leaving: "you are never locked in. You can export your code, migrate your data, self-host parts or all of your stack, or move to another provider at any time." The same page lists what you take on if you do leave, including "Implementing authentication and enforcing data isolation policies".

## What we measured on ObjectStack

We ran three checks on the open-source CRM example that ships with ObjectStack 18.5.0. It is a demonstration app, not a customer system.

1. **An AI change, reviewed as a before-and-after.** An AI agent (the one writing this post) changed one word in the app's files so that sales reps could delete deals. The platform's first check passed: both settings are valid, and only your company knows which one is right. The build then refused to finish and printed one line, "the sales role gains delete on deals", until a person accepted the change. Accepting it rewrote one line of the app's saved access table, and that one-line before-and-after is what a reviewer signs. The mechanism is explained in [How do you catch an AI's silent permission change before it ships?](/en/blog/catch-ai-silent-permission-change-access-matrix-gate/)
2. **A permission the app enforces.** We left the change out and started the app. A sales rep's attempt to delete a deal was refused. An AI agent connected with a key minted on a sales-rep account tried the same delete and was refused twice: first because the request did not carry a confirmation that a person had agreed to a permanent delete, then, with the confirmation, because sales reps may not delete deals.
3. **An audit row for an agent action.** The same agent then moved the deal from "prospecting" to "qualification", which sales reps may do. The audit log gained one row with the agent's account, the deal, and the stage before and after. The two refused deletes left no row; they appear only in the server's log. Reading the audit log is itself restricted: the sales rep's request for it was refused. How read access is recorded, and the limits of that record, are in [Can you prove who opened a customer record, including an AI agent?](/en/blog/prove-who-opened-a-customer-record-view-auditing/)

## What ObjectStack does not do

- **It does not judge whether a change is right.** The first check passed the delete permission without comment. The build stopped only because this example app keeps an access table. A new app does not have one until you switch it on, and the CI file in a new project runs the first check, not the build.
- **It does not stop the agent from accepting its own change.** Accepting is one command that anyone, or anything, with access to the files can run. It becomes a review only when that one-line before-and-after goes through your pull-request process with a person's approval.
- **It does not record refused attempts in the audit log.** Both refused deletes are in the server's log, which is a different place with different retention.
- **It does not know an agent from a person.** The audit row names the account the agent's key was minted on. Nothing on the row says "AI". Give each agent its own account, or the log will truthfully blame a person.
- **It does not keep every change in files.** A running environment can hold edits made through its admin screens or its API, layered over the files: in the platform's own words, "Writes always route to the topmost writable layer (the overlay)." Which changes must go through the files is a policy you set.
- **It is younger than both vendors.** ObjectStack's own comparison page says it plainly: "ObjectStack is younger; expect to build more of it yourself."

## How to choose

**Retool** if your apps sit on databases you already run, your builders are technical, and your auditors ask what users did. Budget for Enterprise if you want every change to go through a pull request.

**Lovable** if business teams build the apps, speed is the point, and the data can live in a back end Lovable hosts or one you move it to later. Budget for Enterprise if you want an admin to approve before an app goes live, and decide who will read the database rules the AI writes.

**Neither, yet,** if the person who signs for the app must read who-can-do-what as a short table rather than as code, and must see every change to it before it takes effect. That is the gap ObjectStack is built for, with the limits listed above.

Whichever you choose, ask the vendor three questions and keep the answers:

1. When the AI changes who can see or do what, what exactly does the approver see before users get it?
2. Can the person who approves be someone other than the person who made the change?
3. Which log answers "what did this user, or this agent, do to that record", and for how long is it kept?

### 1. The AI's change, the build's refusal, the reviewed diff

The edit, on line 50 of `src/security/sales-positions.ts`:

```diff
-    crm_opportunity: { allowRead: true, allowCreate: true, allowEdit: true, allowDelete: false },
+    crm_opportunity: { allowRead: true, allowCreate: true, allowEdit: true, allowDelete: true },
```

`npx os validate` printed `✓ Validation passed` with four unrelated field warnings and exited 0. `npx os compile` ran the same 68 author-time rules and then:

```text
  → Checking access-matrix snapshot (ADR-0090 D6)...
  ✗ Access matrix drift (1 change) — capability changes must be reviewed
  • 'crm_sales_user' gains delete on 'crm_opportunity'
  If intended, re-run with --update-access-matrix and commit the snapshot — its diff IS the review artifact.
compile exit 1
```

Accepting it, `npx os compile --update-access-matrix`, exited 0 with `Access matrix snapshot written to access-matrix.json (ADR-0090 D6) — review the diff.` The snapshot's diff:

```diff
@@ -51,7 +51,7 @@
       "create": true,
       "read": true,
       "edit": true,
-      "delete": false,
+      "delete": true,
       "viewAllRecords": false,
       "modifyAllRecords": false,
       "sharingModel": "public_read_write"
```

The edit was then reverted, so the app below runs with the original permission set.

### 4. A new project has no access table

The `create-objectstack@18.5.0` package, which `npm create objectstack@latest` runs today, contains no `access-matrix.json` in its template, and the template's CI workflow runs `pnpm validate`, not the build. The first `os compile --update-access-matrix` in a new project is the opt-in.
